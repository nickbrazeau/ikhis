L52:   * cite41†Metrics details L53: ## Abstract
L54: During activation, T cells undergo extensive gene expression changes that shape the properties of cells to exert their effector function. Understanding the regulation of this process could help explain how genetic variants predispose to immune diseases. Here, we mapped genetic effects on gene expression (expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs)) using single-cell transcriptomics.
L55: We profiled 655,349 CD4^{+} T cells, capturing transcriptional states of unstimulated cells and three time points of cell activation in 119 healthy individuals. This identified 38 cell clusters, including transient clusters that were only present at individual time points of activation. We found 6,407 genes whose expression was correlated with genetic variation, of which 2,265 (35%) were dynamically regulated during activation.
L56: Furthermore, 127 genes were regulated by variants associated with immune-mediated diseases, with significant enrichment for dynamic effects. Our results emphasize the importance of studying context-specific gene expression regulation and provide insights into the mechanisms underlying genetic susceptibility to immune-mediated diseases.

L75: ## Main
L76: Translating variants from genome-wide association studies (GWASs) to function provides insights into disease biology and improves treatment options^{cite48†1 }. Disease-associated variants from GWASs are enriched within active chromatin regions^{cite49†2 ,cite50†3 }, implicating regulation of gene expression. These effects can be discovered using expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), which link variants to gene expression changes^{cite51†4 }.
L77: However, most currently available eQTL maps use bulk tissues and thus fail to capture gene expression dynamics, such as changes associated with a developmental stage^{cite52†5 ,cite53†6 } or external stimulus^{cite54†7 ,cite55†8 } in a given cell type^{cite56†9 ,cite57†10 }. Mapping dynamic gene expression changes at a single-cell level could overcome these limitations and provide insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying disease.
L78: Variants associated with immune-mediated diseases are enriched in enhancers and promoters whose activity is upregulated upon CD4^{+} T cell activation^{cite58†11 ,cite59†12 }. However, CD4^{+} T cells comprise naive cells, which have not yet encountered an antigen, and memory cells, which have previously undergone activation, both of which respond differently to activation^{cite60†13 ,cite61†14 ,cite62†15 }.
L79: Furthermore, memory cells consist of several subpopulations such as central memory (T_{CM}), effector memory (T_{EM}), and effector memory cells re-expressing CD45RA (T_{EMRA}), which differ in proliferative capacity and effector potential^{cite63†16 ,cite64†17 ,cite65†18 }. Additionally, regulatory T cells (T_{reg}), a subset of CD4^{+} T cells, control T cell activation and prevent excessive inflammation. Transcriptionally, these subpopulations form a continuum of phenotypes^{cite66†19 }.
L80: This cellular heterogeneity further complicates interpretation of immune disease-associated variants.
L81: Given the dynamic nature of T cell activation and the heterogeneity of CD4^{+} T cells, we mapped gene expression regulation using single-cell transcriptomes spanning four time points of CD4^{+} T cell activation. We reconstructed activation trajectories for naive and memory CD4^{+} T cells and identified eQTL effects manifesting at different time points and across different subpopulations of cells. We identified 127 genes with colocalizing eQTL and GWAS signals for immune-mediated diseases.
L82: Colocalizing genes were enriched in time-dependent eQTLs. Our data suggest that dysregulation of gene expression during T cell activation could underlie immune disease and emphasize the importance of context-specific gene expression regulation.
L83: ## Results
L84: ### Single-cell response of CD4^{+} T cells to activation
L85: We isolated and stimulated naive and memory CD4^{+} T cells from 119 individuals and performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)^{cite67†20 } (Fig. cite68†1a , Supplementary Tables cite69†1 and cite69†2 and Supplementary Fig. cite70†1a,b ). We profiled cells in resting state, before dividing (16 h), after the first cell division (40 h) and after acquiring effector functions (5 d)^{cite66†19 }. This process resulted in high-quality data for 655,349 cells (cite71†Methods and Supplementary Fig. cite70†1c–g ).

L94: We performed dimensionality reduction and embedding using uniform manifold approximation (UMAP) (ref. ^{cite74†21 }) (cite71†Methods ) and observed that cells separated by time point of stimulation, forming a gradual progression from resting to the most activated cell state (cells collected at 5 d) (Fig. cite68†1b ). This progression was accompanied by changes in activation markers.
L95: For example, an early activation marker, CD69, was upregulated at 16 h but downregulated at later time points, whereas expression of IL2RA, a marker of late activation, peaked at 40 h, remaining present at 5 d (Fig. cite68†1c ). A population of cells localized at the intermediate point between resting and 16 h-stimulated cells (Fig. cite68†1b ), and was composed of cells from the 16 h (74%) and 40 h (26%) time points. We hypothesized that this intermediate group represented an early activation state.
L96: By analyzing cells from these two time points independently, we observed that at each of these time points cells separated into two clear groups, one corresponding to the early activation state (Supplementary Fig. cite70†3 ). Cells in the early activation group expressed fourfold fewer genes compared to other cells at their respective activation time points and showed lower expression of T cell activation markers^{cite66†19 } (Supplementary Fig. cite70†3 ).
L97: Furthermore, they showed a unique profile characterized by high expression of STAT1, IFIT3 and GBP1 (Fig. cite68†1c ). Therefore, these cells represent a distinct, early activation state, and we refer to them as lowly active.
L98: Next, we performed unsupervised clustering of cells throughout the activation time course. This revealed a total of 51 cell clusters, which were merged into 38 cell populations based on their correlated patterns of gene expression (Supplementary Fig. cite70†4 and Methods). This included 25 stable subpopulations consistently detected at multiple time points and 13 transient cell states only detected at specific time points (Fig. cite68†1d and Supplementary Table cite69†3 ).
L99: Stable subpopulations belonged to one of five phenotypes: naive (T_{N}), T_{CM}, T_{EM}, T_{EMRA} and T_{reg} CD4^{+} T cells (Fig. cite68†1d,e ). The memory pool consisted on average of 60% T_{CM}, 30% T_{EM}, 5% T_{reg} and 5% T_{EMRA} (Supplementary Fig. cite70†6a ). The percentage of T_{EM} cells decreased, whereas T_{CM} and T_{EMRA} increased with age (Supplementary Fig. cite70†6b ). We observed no significant differences in subpopulations between sexes (Supplementary Fig. cite70†6c ).
L100: Additionally, we observed transient cell states that were only detected at specific activation time points (Fig. cite68†1d,e ), such as a population of cells expressing high levels of interferon (IFN)-induced genes (e.g., IFI6, IFIT3, ISG15 and MX1) during early activation (Supplementary Fig. cite70†5 ). Another subpopulation expressed high levels of nuclear factor κB response genes (e.g., NFKBID, REL and BCL2A1) (Supplementary Fig. cite70†5 ) and was dominant at midstages of activation.
L101: Additionally, during late activation, we observed a population of mitotic cells and a group of cells expressing high levels of heat shock protein family members (for example HSPA1A, HSPA1B and DNAJB1; Supplementary Fig. cite70†5 ). Notably, heat shock proteins have been implicated in controlling T cell responses to fever^{cite75†22 }. We also observed a subset of T_{EM} cells that upregulated HLA molecules (e.g., HLA-DRA, HLA-DPA1 and HLA-DRB1) during late activation (Supplementary Fig. cite70†5 ).
L102: Importantly, all individuals contributed uniformly to each cluster, with more variability observed in T_{EMRA}, as previously described^{cite76†17 ,cite77†23 } (Supplementary Fig. cite70†5f ).
L103: ### A temporal eQTL map of CD4^{+} T cell activation
L104: To study the genetic regulation of gene expression throughout T cell activation, we performed cis-eQTL mapping. For each time point, we reconstructed average transcriptional profiles per cell type and individual (i.e., pseudobulk transcriptomes) corresponding to T_{N} and T_{M} CD4^{+} T cells (Methods). We detected 1,545–3,006 genes with significant cis-eQTL effects (eGenes) at different activation time points (Fig. cite78†2a ), of which 210–640 eGenes were only detected in individual cell states (Fig.
L105: cite78†2b ). For example, the kinase gene NME4 and the purinoceptor gene P2RX4 only showed effects in T_{M} at 16 h and 40 h of activation, respectively (Fig. cite78†2c ). The multivariate adaptive shrinkage (mashR) method^{cite79†24 } revealed a higher level of eQTL sharing across cell types within the same time point (Supplementary Fig. cite70†7a ) than across different time points, suggesting that eQTL effect sizes change throughout activation.
L106: We also observed a high replicability (0.67–0.75) of our results with publicly available CD4^{+} T cell eQTLs from bulk RNA sequencing^{cite80†25 ,cite81†26 } (Supplementary Fig. cite70†7c ). However, eQTL sharing was reduced when taking into account both the direction and the magnitude of eQTL effects (0.28–0.34) (Supplementary Fig. cite70†7c ), suggesting that effect sizes might differ between different transcriptomic profiling strategies, naive and memory cells and across T cell activation time points.

L118: To gain a more granular view of gene expression regulation throughout T cell activation, we mapped eQTLs in the 38 cell populations (Fig. cite68†1 ). As expected, we observed a high overlap between eGenes detected in different subpopulations (Fig. cite78†2d ). Nevertheless, T_{EM} and cells expressing HLA genes (T_{EM} HLA^{+}) had a higher number of specific eGenes (62%–97%) compared to other populations, suggesting that they are more transcriptionally different than other subsets.
L119: Small subpopulations, such as T_{EMRA}, yielded a low number of eGenes (3–23), which suggested that the statistical power to detect eGenes correlates with the number of cells profiled (R^{2} = 0.82, P = 4.8 × 10^{−10}) (Fig. cite78†2e ). Indeed, when we subsampled different numbers of T_{CM} cells and repeatedly performed eQTL mapping, we observed that eGene discovery increased proportionally to the number of cells analyzed (Supplementary Fig. cite70†7b ).
L120: Despite this, we identified subpopulation-specific eGenes absent from the whole T_{N} or T_{M} populations. For example, 56–153 eGenes (10%–16%) were found in the subpopulation of naive cells characterized by expression of high levels of IFN-induced genes, but these effects were absent from the whole activated T_{N} cells (Fig. cite78†2f ). Similarly, 47–528 (13%–31%) eGenes were detected in either of the two largest T_{M} subpopulations (T_{CM} and T_{EM}), but not in the whole T_{M} population (Fig.
L121: cite78†2g ). For example, GNPDA1 was an eGene in T_{CM}, but not in T_{EM} or in whole memory cells (Fig. cite78†2g ). These genes were only detected as eQTLs in specific cell clusters, and we observed that many were not detected in the Database of Immune Cell eQTLs (DICE) data set, which includes different subsets of CD4^{+} T cells in resting and stimulated states^{cite81†26 }.
L122: For example, VAMP8 and AIMP1 eQTLs (T_{CM}-specific P_{adj} = 5 × 10^{−4} and P_{adj} = 1.04 × 10^{−4}, respectively) and RNF168 (specific to IFN-expressing cell cluster P_{adj} = 6.2 ×10^{−3}) were not detected in any of the T cell populations in DICE. Therefore, as more studies emerge, the power to detect cluster-specific eQTLs will increase, uncovering eQTLs that were previously undetected in bulk tissues.
L123: ### Cell-type-specific coexpression gene modules
L124: We next sought to understand which transcriptional programs shape the T cell response to activation and whether eGenes regulate T cell functions. We computed pairwise gene expression correlations^{cite84†27 } of 11,130 highly expressed and variable genes across 106 individuals and the 38 identified cell populations (Fig. cite85†3a , Supplementary Fig. cite70†8 and Methods). We identified 12 gene modules that represent key cellular functions involved in T cell activation (Fig.
L125: cite85†3b and Supplementary Table cite69†4 and cite69†5 ). For example, module 4 contained genes involved in the regulation of cell cycle checkpoints and DNA repair and was highly expressed at 40 h and 5 d after activation, consistent with the timing of the first cell division^{cite86†28 }. Furthermore, module 11 included genes whose expression peaked in lowly active and 16 h-stimulated cells and remained high at later time points.
L126: These genes were involved in IFN-induced antiviral mechanisms such as OAS and ISG15-signaling, which are induced rapidly upon viral infection.

L135: In addition to separating genes by temporal dynamics, the coexpression networks also highlighted subpopulation-specific gene expression modules, corresponding to effector T cell functions. For example, genes involved in cytokine secretion and interleukin signaling were highly expressed in T_{EM} and T_{EMRA}, but not T_{CM} or T_{N} cells (Fig. cite85†3a,b ), reflecting the potential of T_{EM} and T_{EMRA} cells to respond quickly^{cite65†18 ,cite66†19 }.
L136: Consistent with this observation, T_{EM} and T_{EMRA} showed high expression of T cell receptor (TCR)-induced genes (i.e., targets of ZAP-70 and downstream of CD3 zeta chain phosphorylation) at an earlier stage of activation, whereas other subpopulations did not express these genes until 40 h after stimulation (Fig. cite85†3a,b ). Furthermore, we observed that module 12, which included genes important for cytotoxic function and chemokine signaling, was most highly expressed in T_{EMRA} (Fig. cite85†3a ).
L137: This cytotoxic capacity distinguishes T_{EMRA} from other T cell subpopulations.
L138: Next, using a permutation strategy (Methods), we showed that eGenes detected in activated T cells were particularly enriched in modules 2 (metabolism), 3 (cell division) and 9 (immune processes) (Fig. cite85†3c ). In contrast, eGenes detected in resting cells showed strongest enrichment in module 6 (RNA metabolism and herpes infection) (Fig. cite85†3c ).
L139: Finally, we observed that eQTL effect sizes, as well as log-transformed allelic fold changes^{cite89†29 }, negatively correlated with the centrality values of their corresponding eGenes in the coexpression network; that is, eGenes with larger eQTL effects were less connected in the network (Fig. cite85†3d and Supplementary Fig. cite70†8b,c ). This suggests that genes at the edges of the coexpression network are more tolerant to variation in gene expression.
L140: ### Modeling of time-dependent eQTL effects
L141: Previous studies showed that eQTLs can be context specific^{cite54†7 ,cite90†30 }. Therefore, we assessed the role of genetic variation on the regulation of gene expression dynamics throughout T cell activation (dynamic eQTLs). We used trajectory inference^{cite91†31 } (Methods) to model activation time as a continuous variable (Fig. cite92†4a ).
L142: The inferred trajectory agreed with the time points profiled experimentally, and T cell activation markers such as IL7R (reduced expression upon activation), CD69 (early activation) and IL2RA (early and late activation) (Fig. cite92†4b ) also followed their expected expression patterns. In total, we identified 5,090 genes for which expression changed as a function of pseudotime (Supplementary Table cite69†6 ).
L143: For example, IRF1 and TOP2A were respectively downregulated and upregulated at late stages of activation (Fig. cite92†4b ). Dynamically regulated genes were enriched in pathways related to T cell activation, such as DNA replication and regulation of cell cycle, mRNA transcription and processing, protein translation, signaling downstream of the TCR and signaling by interleukins (Supplementary Table cite69†7 ).
L144: Finally, we observed that T_{M} cells were characterized by lower pseudotime values than T_{N} cells sampled at the same time points. This is a consequence of T_{M} cells showing a shorter activation path, likely reflecting a faster response.

L154: To model dynamic eQTLs, we divided the pseudotime trajectory into ten bins and averaged the expression of genes per individual in each bin (Methods). Splitting the trajectory enabled us to control for the numbers of cells and therefore to reliably estimate mean gene expression values. We then used mixed models to identify eQTLs for which the effect size changed as a function of activation time (Fig. cite92†4c and Methods).
L155: We identified 2,265 genes with dynamic eQTL effects, which comprised 34% of eGenes in our data set (Supplementary Table cite69†8 and Supplementary Fig. cite70†9a ). We used a permutation-based strategy to validate that this method was well calibrated (Methods and Supplementary Fig. cite70†9b ). We applied both linear and quadratic models and observed that most eQTLs followed linear dynamics (74% and 76% in T_{N} and T_{M} cells, respectively; Fig. cite92†4e ).
L156: However, for 502 and 495 genes in naive and memory cells, respectively, we detected a nonlinear interaction with activation pseudotime. For example, GBP7 and CFLAR demonstrated eQTL effects only upon activation, and their magnitude peaked at midstages of the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. cite92†4d ). In contrast, the magnitude of an eQTL for SERINC5 peaked at early stages of the trajectory and diminished throughout activation (Fig.
L157: cite92†4d ), whereas an eQTL for the INF-α-inducible gene IFI27L1 showed an effect size that linearly increased along the activation trajectory.
L158: Finally, linear eQTLs were enriched in metabolic pathways, whereas nonlinear eQTLs were enriched in both metabolic and immune processes (e.g., T cell proliferation and leukocyte degranulation) (Fig. cite92†4f ). This suggests that for many immune genes, genetic regulation is only evident during specific stages of T cell activation.
L159: ### Colocalization at GWAS loci identifies immune disease genes
L160: We obtained summary statistics for 13 immune-mediated diseases available in the GWAS catalog^{cite95†32 } (Methods) and tested for colocalization^{cite96†33 ,cite97†34 } (Methods) with the eQTLs mapped to T_{N,} T_{M} and the subpopulations. We identified 471 unique colocalizations (PP4 > 0.8), corresponding to 247 GWAS loci for 11 diseases and 314 SNP–gene pairs (Supplementary Tables cite69†9 and cite69†10 ). This enabled us to prioritize 127 candidate disease-causal genes (Fig. cite98†5a ).
L161: Importantly, 77 (60%) colocalizing genes were detected upon activation and would have been missed by profiling only steady state ex vivo cells. Out of those, 47 (37%) were captured specifically in later time points of activation (40 h + 5 d) (Fig. cite98†5b ). This finding is important, as previous eQTL studies have relied on either resting cells or a single, usually early activation time point^{cite49†2 ,cite81†26 }.
